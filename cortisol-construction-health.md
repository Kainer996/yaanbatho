# Chronic Stress, Cortisol Dysregulation, and Mental Health Risk in UK Quarrying and Construction Workers

## A Scientific and Occupational Health Briefing for the Health and Safety Department

**Prepared for:** Tarmac Health and Safety Department
**Subject:** The physiological and psychological impact of verbal mistreatment, abusive supervision and chronic interpersonal stress on workers in quarrying and construction operations, with reference to UK suicide and ill-health data
**Status:** Internal briefing / evidence summary

---

## Executive summary

UK construction workers — including quarry operatives — die by suicide at a rate approximately **3.7 times** the national male average, with ONS data showing **355 suicides** among skilled construction and building trade workers in England and Wales in 2024 alone. Industry-wide surveys show **97% of construction workers report stress, 87% anxiety and 70% depression** within a 12-month period.

The biological pathway connecting daily verbal mistreatment on a worksite to long-term mental ill-health and elevated suicide risk is **well established in peer-reviewed literature**. Repeated psychosocial threats — being shouted at over a radio, humiliated, or spoken to disrespectfully — activate the **hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis** and cause **chronic elevation of cortisol**. Sustained over months and years, this produces measurable damage to cardiovascular, metabolic, immune and neurological systems (a state called **allostatic overload**), and is causally implicated in major depressive disorder, generalised anxiety disorder and suicidal ideation.

Under the **Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974** and the **Management of Health and Safety at Work Regulations 1999**, the employer has a legal duty to assess and control these risks. The **HSE Management Standards** explicitly identify *Relationships* — defined as "promoting positive working to avoid conflict and dealing with unacceptable behaviour" — as one of the six primary work-related stressors that must be managed. Verbal mistreatment over operational radios, where present, is a controllable hazard on par with dust exposure, noise or working at height.

This document sets out the underlying science, the UK industry data, and the legal and practical case for treating interpersonal conduct as a Tier 1 occupational health hazard within Tarmac's quarry operations.

## 1. The problem on the ground

In a working quarry, two-way radio is the principal channel of supervision and inter-team communication. Unlike a face-to-face conversation, a radio transmission is:

- **One-to-many** — every operator on the channel hears it
- **Asymmetric** — the recipient cannot easily reply or push back without being heard by everyone
- **Repetitive** — exposure recurs throughout an 8–12 hour shift
- **Inescapable** — the worker is required to monitor the radio to do the job safely

When supervision delivered through that channel is hostile, demeaning or abusive, it functions as a **chronic, unavoidable psychosocial stressor**. The worker cannot leave, cannot remove the headset, and cannot respond. This is precisely the type of stressor that the human stress system handles most poorly.

Anecdotally, workers in this environment report carrying anxiety and low mood through the entire shift and into their home lives. The remainder of this paper sets out why that is not "just the way it is" — it is a measurable physiological injury that compounds over time.

## 2. The biology: how words become physical harm

### 2.1 The HPA axis and the cortisol response

The **hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis** is the body's central stress-response system. When the brain perceives a threat — physical, social or psychological — the hypothalamus releases corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), which prompts the pituitary to release adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), which in turn drives the adrenal glands to release **cortisol** [1].

Cortisol is essential and adaptive in short bursts: it mobilises glucose, sharpens attention, and suppresses non-urgent functions like digestion and immune activity to handle the emergency. In a healthy system, once the threat passes, cortisol falls and a **negative feedback loop** returns the axis to baseline.

### 2.2 What goes wrong with chronic activation

Repeated, unresolved psychosocial stress disrupts this feedback loop. The literature shows two consistent findings in chronically stressed populations:

1. **Sustained hypercortisolaemia** — cortisol stays elevated outside its normal diurnal rhythm
2. **Impaired feedback inhibition** — the "off switch" stops working, so the axis cannot reset [2][3]

This is not a metaphor. It is measured directly in saliva, blood and hair cortisol assays in occupational and clinical studies.

### 2.3 Downstream consequences of chronic cortisol elevation

Sustained cortisol elevation is causally linked to:

- **Depression and anxiety disorders.** Dysregulation of the HPA axis with elevated cortisol is "strongly associated with the onset and progression of major mental disorders, including depression, bipolar disorder, and psychosis" [2]. Elevated cortisol depletes serotonin, the principal mood-regulating neurotransmitter [2].
- **Hippocampal damage and cognitive decline.** Chronic HPA dysfunction generates oxidative stress and neuroinflammation, producing structural and functional changes in the hippocampus — the brain region responsible for emotional regulation and memory [4].
- **Cardiovascular disease.** Chronic glucocorticoid and catecholamine secretion increases the risk of hypertension, atherosclerosis and myocardial infarction [5].
- **Metabolic disease.** Cortisol drives visceral fat deposition, insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes [5].
- **Immune dysregulation.** Initial immunosuppression is followed by a chronic low-grade inflammatory state that contributes to multiple disease pathways [4].

### 2.4 Allostatic load: the cumulative bill

The cumulative wear and tear on the body from chronic stress is termed **allostatic load**. It is measured across four biological systems — cardiovascular, metabolic, inflammatory and neuroendocrine — and is now treated as a clinical biomarker [5].

A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis found that high allostatic load was associated with **increased all-cause mortality (hazard ratios 1.08–2.75)** and increased cardiovascular mortality across the majority of studies examined [5]. In plain terms: chronic workplace stress shortens lives, and it is measurable.

## 3. Verbal mistreatment and abusive supervision: the evidence

### 3.1 Workplace incivility and verbal abuse

A 2024 review in the *Journal of Public Health* found that workplace incivility was associated with **statistically significant differences in 32 of 33** indicators of health, well-being, stress and recovery [6]. The mechanism is the chronic stress response described above: incivility releases adrenaline, noradrenaline and cortisol on a near-daily basis.

Verbal abuse specifically — being shouted at, criticised hostilely or humiliated — correlates with anger, depression, anxiety, guilt, humiliation and helplessness [7]. Verbal abuse from co-workers and supervisors produces **higher emotional exhaustion than abuse from external sources** [7], because it cannot be escaped without losing one's job.

### 3.2 Abusive supervision and suicide risk

"Abusive supervision" is the academic term for the exact pattern described on the shop floor: a manager who routinely demeans, shouts at, or publicly humiliates subordinates. Research findings are blunt:

- Abusive supervision **directly exacerbates suicidal ideation** (β = 0.160, 95% CI [0.038, 0.281], p = 0.009) [8].
- The pathway runs through **depression**, **thwarted belongingness**, and **perceived burdensomeness** — three of the core constructs in the Interpersonal Psychological Theory of Suicide [8].
- A meta-analysis of workplace bullying confirms a positive association with suicidal ideation and behaviour [9].

This is a dose-response relationship. The more frequently a worker is exposed to abusive supervision, the higher their depression scores and the higher their suicidal ideation scores.

### 3.3 Psychosocial Safety Climate

"**Psychosocial Safety Climate (PSC)**" is the technical term for an organisation's policies, practices and procedures for protecting worker psychological health. A landmark study by Dollard and Bakker (2010) established PSC as a **lead indicator** of bullying, harassment, psychological health problems and engagement [10]. Sites with weak PSC predictably develop the conditions described in this paper; sites with strong PSC do not.

A 2023 CDC/NIOSH science blog identified psychosocial hazards — including bullying and verbal abuse — as **systematically overlooked** in the construction industry, despite being among the strongest predictors of worker depression, substance use and suicide [11].

## 5. The legal and regulatory position

### 5.1 Statutory duties

Under the **Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974, s.2(1)**, every employer has a duty to ensure, so far as is reasonably practicable, the **health, safety and welfare** of all employees. "Health" includes mental health. This was confirmed by HSE in *Tackling work-related stress using the Management Standards approach* [15].

The **Management of Health and Safety at Work Regulations 1999** require employers to undertake a **suitable and sufficient risk assessment** of the risks to employee health, including risks from work-related stress.

### 5.2 The HSE Management Standards

The HSE Management Standards provide the recognised framework for discharging those duties. They cover six areas of work design [15][16]:

1. **Demands** — workload, work patterns, work environment
2. **Control** — autonomy over the way work is done
3. **Support** — encouragement, sponsorship, resources from the organisation, line management and colleagues
4. **Relationships** — *"promoting positive working to avoid conflict and dealing with unacceptable behaviour"*
5. **Role** — clarity of role, absence of role conflict
6. **Change** — how organisational change is managed and communicated

Hostile radio communication and abusive supervision sit squarely under **Relationships** and **Support**. They are explicitly within scope of the standards Tarmac is expected to meet.

### 5.3 Enforcement context

HSE has powers to issue **Improvement Notices** and **Prohibition Notices** in respect of work-related stress where an employer has failed to assess or control the risk. While such notices have historically been rare in the stress domain, the regulatory direction of travel — particularly given the 22.1 million days lost in 2024/25 — is towards greater enforcement, not less.

## 6. Why this is a quarrying issue specifically

Generic construction risk factors apply at quarries, but several are **amplified**:

- **Radio-mediated supervision** is the default communication channel, multiplying the audience for any humiliating exchange.
- **Geographic isolation** of many UK quarry sites limits social support networks and access to mental health services.
- **Lone or small-team working** within large sites reduces buffering by peer support — a known protective factor [17].
- **Plant operation** (loaders, dump trucks, crushers, conveyors) demands sustained vigilance; cortisol-driven cognitive impairment is a **direct safety hazard**, not just a welfare issue. Stress leads to shortcuts; depression reduces situational awareness [18]. Both are mechanisms by which mental ill-health translates into physical incidents — exactly the territory H&S exists to prevent.
- **Masculine help-seeking norms** in heavy industry suppress disclosure, meaning the visible cases are the tip of a much larger iceberg.

In other words: a quarry is not a softer environment than a building site. It is, by several measures, a harder one.

## 7. Recommendations

The following are evidence-based actions that align with the HSE Management Standards and would materially reduce psychosocial risk on Tarmac quarry sites.

### 7.1 Immediate (0–3 months)

1. **Site-level stress risk assessment** under the HSE Management Standards Indicator Tool, covering all six standards, conducted anonymously to permit honest reporting.
2. **Radio communication code of conduct**, stating clearly that the radio is an operational channel and that personal criticism, shouting and humiliation are unacceptable. Treat breaches as conduct issues with a defined escalation path.
3. **Anonymous reporting channel** for psychosocial concerns, separate from line management, with confidentiality guarantees.
4. **Brief mental health awareness session** for all site personnel — already delivered free or at low cost by **Mates in Mind**, the construction industry's specialist charity [14].

### 7.2 Medium-term (3–12 months)

5. **Mental Health First Aid (MHFA)** training for a defined proportion of supervisors and operatives, to provide on-site peer support.
6. **Supervisor and management training** on psychologically safe leadership, including recognising and avoiding abusive supervision behaviours. Less than 10% of construction line managers can currently conduct a stress risk assessment [14] — closing this gap is a quick win.
7. **Inclusion of psychosocial indicators** (sickness absence categorised by cause, turnover, near-miss reports tagged for human-factors causes) in monthly H&S KPI reporting.
8. **Employee Assistance Programme (EAP)** awareness campaign with on-site posters, payslip inserts and toolbox talks. Confirm that all operatives, including agency and contractor staff, can access it.

### 7.3 Long-term (12+ months)

9. **Psychosocial Safety Climate audit** across all UK Tarmac sites, benchmarked annually.
10. **Leadership accountability**: include psychosocial safety metrics in line manager appraisals, on the same footing as LTI rates.
11. **Industry collaboration** with the Mineral Products Association, Mates in Mind and HSE on a quarrying-specific mental health programme — there is currently no such programme and Tarmac is well placed to lead it.

---

## 8. Conclusion

The case set out above is not based on opinion or workplace folklore. It is based on:

- **ONS mortality data** showing UK construction workers die by suicide at 3.7 times the male average
- **Endocrinology and neuroscience literature** establishing the causal pathway from psychosocial stress to HPA-axis dysfunction, cortisol dysregulation, depression, and physical disease
- **Occupational psychology research** linking abusive supervision and verbal mistreatment directly to suicidal ideation
- **HSE statutory guidance** that places interpersonal conduct squarely within the employer's legal duty of care

The men working in Tarmac's quarries are subject — on the evidence available — to a chronic occupational hazard that is at present **uncontrolled and largely unmeasured**. The hazard is hostile interpersonal conduct, principally via the radio. The harm is depression, anxiety, cardiovascular disease and, at the extreme end, suicide.

These are **preventable** outcomes. The Management Standards exist because the science is settled. What is required now is the same rigour applied to psychosocial risk that Tarmac already applies to dust, noise, vibration and falls from height.

The status quo is not neutral. It is costing lives.
